Evaluating AI Output

Trust but verify. How to know when AI is confidently wrong.

← MasterClass Home
Phase 4 · Stay critical
⏱ Time & Energy
12–15 min · any energy · You’ll need: a recent AI output you want to sanity-check
The core problem: AI writes fluent, confident sentences whether it knows the answer or not. There’s no visible difference between “correct” and “completely made up.” Your job is to read with suspicion when it counts.

The trust-but-verify framework

🔬 Why this works
Large language models hallucinate — produce confident, plausible, false statements — in 3-27% of factual claims, depending on domain (Huang et al., 2023). Verification offloads the “is this true” check to a system, not your memory.
☁️ Low energy version
Just ask one question of every output: “What would make this wrong?” If you can’t answer, you can’t verify it. Slow down or use a different source.

The framework is three questions, in this order:

📘 The three checks
StepQuestionIf yes…
1. StakesDoes being wrong here cost me real time, money, or trust?Verify before using. If no, skip the rest.
2. SourceCould this fact be checked in a known place I trust?Check that one place. Don’t trust AI for the answer.
3. PatternDoes the output match patterns I know are usually true?Lower confidence, but still verify the specific claim.
The most important step is #1. Most AI use is low-stakes — brainstorming, drafting, summaries. Save your scrutiny budget for the high-stakes outputs.

The seven red flags

Run through these when stakes are high. If you spot two or more in one output, treat it as a draft, not an answer.

🚩 1. Confident-sounding nonsense
Authoritative tone with no caveats on a topic where any expert would hedge. Real experts say “it depends” a lot. AI rarely does unless you ask.
🚩 2. Invented citations
Specific authors, dates, journals, or URLs that look perfect. AI invents convincing-looking sources constantly. Always click the link. If it 404s, the source is fake.
🚩 3. Circular reasoning
“X is true because it’s widely accepted that X.” Watch for “research shows” or “experts agree” with no specific researchers, no specific findings.
🚩 4. Numbers that match too neatly
Round, satisfying percentages (“70% of users…”) with no source. Real stats are uglier. Specific-looking precision with no citation is a red flag.
🚩 5. Anachronisms
References to versions, products, or events from after the model’s training cutoff. If it confidently describes something that just shipped, double-check — it might be guessing.
🚩 6. “According to” without details
“According to a 2022 study” with no journal, no authors, no link. If AI doesn’t name the study, it probably doesn’t know one exists.
🚩 7. Hedging in the wrong direction
AI hedges on simple facts (“the capital might be…”) and asserts on hard ones (“the optimal architecture is…”). When the confidence-level feels mismatched to the question, get suspicious.

The 60-second checklist

For a busy worker. Stick this on a tab. Run it when stakes are real.

✅ Before I send/use this output
  1. Stakes check. Will being wrong cost me anything? If no, ship it.
  2. Specific facts. Underline names, dates, numbers, URLs. Verify each one externally.
  3. Click every link. Fake URLs are the #1 hallucination. Real links resolve.
  4. Read it aloud. Hallucinations often have a fluent-but-empty quality your ear catches faster than your eye.
  5. Ask “how would I know?” For each major claim — what would I need to see to believe it? Can I see that?
  6. One human check. For high-stakes work, run it past one person who’d know.
⭐ What good looks like
You can defend every specific claim in the output. Not because AI said it — because you checked, or because the stakes are low enough that you don’t need to.

The verify-with-AI trick

You can use AI to verify AI. It’s not foolproof, but it catches obvious problems.

✏️ Red-team your own output
Verify promptI'm going to paste an answer from another AI. Your job is to red-team it.

Tell me:
  1. What specific claims are factually checkable?
  2. Which claims sound confident but might be wrong?
  3. Are any cited sources, names, or dates suspicious?
  4. What's missing that should be there?
  5. If you had to bet $100 something here is wrong, what would you bet on?

Be specific. Don't be polite. Don't summarize.

Here's the output to red-team:

[PASTE THE OUTPUT]
Use a different model than the one that generated the original. Two different models hallucinating the same wrong fact is much less likely than one.
📘 When to skip all this
Skip verification when: brainstorming, free-writing, drafting something you’ll heavily edit, learning a topic (where you’ll Google anyway), or formatting/styling existing content. Verification is for facts that’ll be acted on.
