Advanced Prompt Craft

Three patterns that take you past the 4-element framework.

← MasterClass Home
Phase 3–4 · Deeper craft
Prereqs: You’ve done prompt basics and used the 4-element framework at least a few times. Time: 20–30 min. Energy: Medium (focus required).
⏱ Time & Energy
20–30 min · medium energy · You’ll need: Claude.ai + a real task you’d normally avoid
Why three patterns? The 4-element framework gets you to 70% quality fast. These three patterns get you the last 30% — when you actually care about the output.

Pattern 1: Few-Shot Prompting

🔬 Why this works
Models learn the pattern you want from 2–5 worked examples better than from rules describing the pattern (Brown et al., 2020). Show, then ask.
☁️ Low energy version
Pick ONE recurring task. Find 2 examples you already wrote. Paste them in. Ask AI to do the next one in the same style. Done.

You’re writing weekly status updates at Smartsheet. You’ve described them six different ways in the prompt and the AI still misses the tone. Stop describing. Show.

✏️ Few-shot template — status updates
Few-shot promptYou are a status-update writer for a senior PM at Smartsheet.
Match the tone, structure, and length of these examples exactly.

Example 1:
Week of Mar 3 — Sheets perf
- Shipped: P95 read latency from 480ms to 210ms after index pass
- Next: review wide-row pagination, partner with infra on cache layer
- Risk: none new

Example 2:
Week of Mar 10 — Sheets perf
- Shipped: nothing visible; spent the week on the wide-row review
- Next: ship pagination behind flag, start cache spike
- Risk: cache spike likely slips a week; not blocking

Now write the update for this week:
- I finished the cache layer spike, it works but adds 60ms tail latency
- I'm starting on the rollout plan
- I'm worried we picked the wrong eviction strategy
The AI mimics your terseness, your three-bucket structure (Shipped/Next/Risk), even your habit of saying “nothing visible.” That’s the trick.
⭐ What good looks like
Output reads like you wrote it. If it’s still off, your examples weren’t representative — add a third example, or pick better ones. Two strong examples beat ten mediocre ones.

Pattern 2: Chain-of-Thought

🔬 Why this works
Asking AI to think step-by-step before answering reduces hallucinations on multi-step reasoning by 20–40% (Wei et al., 2022). It trades speed for accuracy. Use it situationally — today's reasoning models (Opus 4.x, GPT-5 series, Gemini 3.x "thinking") already reason step-by-step internally, so the line is mostly redundant there and most useful with faster, non-reasoning models or for forcing visible working.
☁️ Low energy version
When it helps, add one line: “Think through this step by step before answering.” On a reasoning model you can usually skip it — it’s already doing this. Reach for it when the answer comes back too fast and wrong.

Use this for: analyzing data, comparing options, debugging logic, anything where “just give me the answer” usually gives you a confidently wrong answer.

✏️ CoT template — data analysis
Chain-of-thought promptYou are a careful data analyst. Before answering, walk through:
  1. What is the question actually asking?
  2. What data do I have, and what's missing?
  3. What's the simplest analysis that answers it?
  4. What could go wrong with that analysis?
  5. THEN: give your answer, with the caveats from step 4.

Question: Our weekly active users dropped 8% last week.
Is this a real signal or noise?

Data: WAU for the last 12 weeks: 41k, 42k, 40k, 43k, 44k, 45k,
43k, 46k, 45k, 47k, 48k, 44k. Last week was the 44k.
The model will reason out loud, notice the variance is normally ±3k, and conclude this is likely noise — instead of confidently saying “yes, churn problem.”
📘 When to skip CoT
Quick lookups, drafting, brainstorming. CoT slows AI down and costs more tokens. Only use it when being wrong matters more than being fast.

Pattern 3: Iteration

🔬 Why this works
The first answer is rarely the best. Treating AI output as a draft and editing in conversation gets dramatically better results than one-shot prompting (Anthropic Prompt Engineering Guide).
☁️ Low energy version
When you get an answer that’s close-but-not-right, don’t restart. Just reply: “make this X” where X is one specific change. Repeat until done.

Iteration is the cheapest, most under-used technique. The mistake is starting a new conversation every time the answer is wrong. Keep the conversation going.

✏️ Iteration recipe — drafting a doc
Iteration sequenceYou 1: Draft a one-page design doc for adding offline mode to our app.
  Audience: senior engineers. Sections: problem, proposal, risks.

AI: [first draft, probably generic]

You 2: The "problem" section is too abstract. Rewrite it with a
  concrete user scenario — someone on a plane losing 2 hours of work.

AI: [better problem section]

You 3: Risks section is missing the data sync conflict case.
  Add it as the top risk, with two mitigation options.

You 4: Tighten the whole thing. Cut 30% of the words. Keep all sections.

You 5: One more pass — make it sound like an engineer wrote it, not a PM.
Each turn is one specific change. The doc gets sharper every step. One ask per turn is the rule.
⭐ What good looks like
You’re using AI like a writing partner, not a vending machine. The final draft is something neither you nor AI would’ve gotten to alone.

Combine them: the real workflow

These patterns aren’t separate. A real prompt for important work often uses all three:

🧠 All three together — drafting an email you’ve been avoiding
Combined promptYou are a direct, warm email writer. Match my style from these examples:

Example 1: [paste an old email you wrote that you liked]
Example 2: [paste another]

Before drafting, think through:
  1. What is the recipient actually worried about?
  2. What do they need from me in this email?
  3. What's the shortest version that answers both?

Then draft. I'll iterate from there.

The task: I owe my manager an update on the project that's behind.
Context: It's behind because Q1 priorities shifted, not because I dropped it.
I want to acknowledge the slip without sounding defensive.
📘 Mini-cheatsheet
When you want…Reach for…
Output to match a specific styleFew-shot (2–5 examples)
Analysis or multi-step reasoningChain-of-thought (“think step-by-step”)
To turn a draft into something goodIteration (one ask per turn)
To save it for laterAdd to your Prompt Library

Three patterns: few-shot (show, don’t describe), chain-of-thought (think before answering), iteration (one ask per turn).
